Learner field guide · Analyzing Problems & Developing Solutions

Think clearly before you decide confidently.

Critical thinking is a disciplined way to analyse information, test its validity, compare alternatives and reach a reasoned decision. It turns “this feels right” into a conclusion that can be explained, challenged and improved.

5pillars working together

01

Start here

What critical thinking really means

Critical thinking is not constant doubt. It is the ability to think clearly, rationally and independently—using evidence and logic while remaining willing to revise your view.

Analyse

Break the situation apart

Define the problem, distinguish facts from interpretations, and identify the components, stakeholders and constraints.

Evaluate

Judge the information

Ask whether evidence is relevant, credible and sufficient. Compare explanations and look for assumptions or missing context.

Reason

Reach a defensible decision

Connect evidence to conclusions, anticipate consequences, explain the choice and monitor whether it works.

Critical thinkers tend to be…

  • Curious: they seek the “why” behind information.
  • Open-minded: they consider multiple perspectives.
  • Logical: they use evidence and reasoning.
  • Problem-oriented: they make complex issues manageable.

It does not mean…

  • Being negative about every proposal.
  • Ignoring experience, emotion or values.
  • Winning an argument at any cost.
  • Waiting for perfect information before acting.

02

The operating loop

A systematic process

Critical thinking strengthens problem-solving by giving it an inspectable sequence. Each stage has a different job; jumping straight to a solution usually means solving an assumed problem.

  1. 01FrameWhat exactly is happening, for whom, where and when?
  2. 02GatherWhat evidence, context and perspectives are relevant?
  3. 03EvaluateWhat is credible? What is assumption, bias or uncertainty?
  4. 04DecideWhich option best fits the evidence, consequences and values?
  5. 05MonitorDid it work? What changed? What should be revised?

The loop matters. Evaluating effectiveness is part of the decision, not an optional afterthought. Evidence from the result becomes input for the next cycle.

03

The mind examining itself

Bias, intuition and intellectual humility

Human judgement uses shortcuts. They are efficient and often necessary, but they can make the first explanation feel more certain than the evidence permits. Critical thinking does not eliminate bias by memorising a list; it changes the process so that alternative explanations, disconfirming evidence and review become normal.

01

Confirmation bias

We notice and favour information that supports what we already believe.

Repair: actively ask what evidence would show your view is wrong.

02

Anchoring

The first number, estimate or explanation disproportionately shapes later judgement.

Repair: make independent estimates before discussing the first proposal.

03

Availability

Vivid or recent examples feel more common or important than they really are.

Repair: check base rates, longer time periods and representative data.

04

Motivated reasoning

Identity, incentives or desired outcomes subtly influence how strictly we judge evidence.

Repair: state interests, use common criteria and invite independent challenge.

Intellectual humility

Recognise that your information and reasoning may be incomplete. It sounds like: “My current view is X, based on Y; I would revise it if Z appeared.” This is calibrated confidence—not weakness.

Open-mindedness with standards

Giving alternatives a fair hearing does not mean treating every claim as equally credible. Evidence quality, explanatory power and ethical consequences still matter.

A practical habit: before finalising a decision, write the strongest competing explanation and one observation that would distinguish it from your preferred explanation.

03

The central framework

The five pillars

The lesson presents five complementary principles. They are strongest together: sound logic can still mislead if the evidence is thin; a well-supported argument can still fail if it is communicated poorly; a persuasive decision can still be wrong if it ignores ethics.

Logic

Ensures conclusions follow a clear, rational sequence from the evidence.

Example: trace how unclear task assignments contribute to missed deadlines rather than blaming “poor focus.”

Ask: Does the conclusion actually follow?

Argumentation

Structures a claim and supports it with relevant reasons and evidence.

Example: build a case for additional staffing using workload, delay and productivity data.

Ask: What claim am I making, and what supports it?

Rhetoric

Communicates ideas persuasively and appropriately for the audience.

Example: explain a new strategy differently to engineers, managers and customers without changing the evidence.

Ask: How will this audience understand and use it?

Background knowledge

Provides relevant facts, expertise, history and context for the decision.

Example: compare supplier records, industry benchmarks and previous project performance.

Ask: What context would change my interpretation?

Character

Brings ethical reasoning, empathy and respect for different perspectives.

Example: consider labour practices and sustainability as well as supplier price and reliability.

Ask: Is the decision fair, responsible and trustworthy?

Background knowledge supplies context→Logic tests the reasoning→Argumentation builds the case→Rhetoric carries the meaning→Character governs the choice

05

Make reasoning visible

An argument is a structure, not a quarrel

In critical thinking, an argument is a claim supported by reasons. The important part is often the warrant: the rule or assumption that explains why the evidence supports the claim. Argument mapping makes these relationships visible and gives opposing evidence somewhere legitimate to sit.

Claim
Joint planning should be introduced before feature commitments are made.
Supporting evidence

Most schedule slippage begins on work committed before developer estimates and dependencies are recorded.

Counter-evidence

Some well-planned items still slip because requirements change after work starts.

Warrant
If early shared estimates reduce avoidable uncertainty and rework, then joint planning should improve delivery—but it will not control later scope changes.

Three tests for an argument

Acceptability

Can we trust the premises?

Check the source, measurement, context and whether observations are being presented as facts.

Relevance

Do they bear on the claim?

True information may still be irrelevant. Team morale alone does not establish what caused schedule variance.

Sufficiency

Is the support strong enough?

The more consequential the claim, the more evidence, challenge and uncertainty analysis it deserves.

Common reasoning failures

False choice

“Either managers plan the work or developers control everything.” Look for mixed or alternative arrangements.

Hasty generalisation

One failed sprint does not prove the whole process is broken. Check frequency and comparison periods.

Correlation as cause

Two things changing together does not establish the mechanism. Ask what else could generate the pattern.

Moving the target

Changing the success criterion after seeing the result protects a belief from being tested.

Attacking the person

A person’s style or status does not determine whether their evidence is sound.

Appeal to popularity

Agreement can matter for adoption, but the number of believers does not make a factual claim true.

04

Quality of judgement

Move from assertion to evidence

A confident claim is not automatically a strong claim. Better reasoning makes the path from observation to conclusion visible and keeps uncertainty attached to the decision.

LEVEL 1

Opinion

“The team lacks focus.”

LEVEL 2

Observation

“Three deadlines were missed this quarter.”

LEVEL 3

Pattern + context

“Misses cluster where work begins before estimates and owners are agreed.”

LEVEL 4

Testable explanation

“Joint planning and explicit ownership should reduce rework; track variance and missed hand-offs for two sprints.”

Credibility

Who produced the information? How was it gathered? Is there corroboration?

Relevance

Does it address the actual decision, population and time period?

Sufficiency

Is there enough evidence for the confidence and consequences of this decision?

Match the evidence burden to the stakes. A reversible experiment may justify acting with partial evidence. A clinical, safety-critical or irreversible decision demands stronger validation, independent challenge and explicit limits.

07

Critical thinking in an information-rich world

Digital sources, uncertainty and AI

Online information changes the task. A polished page, a confident answer or a chart with precise numbers may still rest on a weak source. Modern critical thinking therefore includes source investigation, uncertainty communication and the ability to use AI without outsourcing judgement.

Lateral reading: investigate the source from outside the source

Professional fact-checkers do not remain on an unfamiliar page and merely inspect its design. They open other tabs to discover who is behind it, what independent sources say, and whether the original claim has been represented accurately.

01

Pause

Do not immediately share, cite or act on the claim.

02

Investigate

Search for the author, organisation, funding and relevant expertise.

03

Trace

Find the original study, dataset, quotation or policy—not a chain of summaries.

04

Corroborate

Compare independent, appropriately expert sources and explain disagreements.

Make uncertainty usable

What is known?Direct observations, measured patterns and well-supported facts.
What is inferred?The explanation linking evidence to the conclusion.
What is unknown?Gaps, limitations, plausible alternatives and missing populations.
What would change the decision?New evidence, a threshold, a failed prediction or an adverse consequence.

Confidence should match evidence quality. “Likely,” “possible” and “uncertain” are useful only when the reader also learns why, what the main alternatives are, and what the uncertainty means for action.

Use AI as a reasoning partner, not an authority

Generative AI can propose explanations, reorganise material and expose questions you have not considered. It can also invent citations, flatten disagreement, reproduce bias and sound equally confident when right or wrong. The learner remains responsible for the claim and its consequences.

STEP 1DefineState the decision, audience and constraints before prompting.
STEP 2GenerateAsk for alternatives, objections and missing information.
STEP 3InterrogateSeparate claims, assumptions and proposed evidence.
STEP 4VerifyCheck important claims against original, trustworthy sources.
STEP 5JudgeApply domain knowledge, ethics and consequence analysis.
STEP 6DiscloseExplain material AI use, uncertainty and human review.

AI-specific warning: asking the same model to “check its answer” is not independent verification. Use external evidence, inspect the original material and involve an appropriately knowledgeable person when the stakes are high.

05

Applied challenge

A software team missing deadlines

The situation

One delay, two competing stories

A development team is launching a feature. Deadlines are missed and tension rises.

Developers say:
insufficient planning creates unrealistic deadlines.
↔
Project managers say:
the team lacks focus and prioritisation.

You must assess the situation, identify the underlying problem and propose a practical way to improve collaboration and productivity.

A disciplined route

Apply all five pillars

  1. Frame: separate deadlines, communication, planning and prioritisation.
  2. Gather: listen to both groups; inspect workflows, estimates, assignments and prior performance.
  3. Analyse: locate where delay enters the process and test both explanations.
  4. Construct: build a recommendation linked to the evidence.
  5. Communicate ethically: make it actionable, fair and respectful of every stakeholder.

A worked response

Diagnosis

Planning happens without enough developer input, producing weak estimates, unclear ownership and low shared commitment.

Intervention

Use regular joint planning, visible tasks and priorities, explicit owners, and a short review of actual versus estimated work.

Measure

Track deadline variance, reopened work, blocked time, unplanned changes and team feedback across several cycles.

Critical extension: a new tool is not automatically a solution. Jira or Trello may improve visibility, but only if roles, planning and decision rules improve too. Otherwise the same disagreement becomes more neatly documented.

Compare with the lesson’s example solution

The source example identifies lack of alignment during planning as the primary issue. It recommends bi-weekly sprint planning involving developers and project managers, a visible project-management tool, and shared Agile training. Its justification is increased transparency, achievable goals, mutual understanding and trackable deadlines.

The stronger version above adds a measurement stage so the team can evaluate whether the intervention actually changes delays and collaboration.

06

Deliberate improvement

Reflect on how you think

Reflection turns an experience into evidence about your own practice. In current learning research this is often described as metacognition: knowing how you tend to think, then deliberately planning, monitoring and evaluating your approach.

Plan

Clarify the goal, what you know, what you need to learn and which strategy fits.

Before: “What would a good answer need to establish?”
→

Monitor

Notice confusion, assumptions, evidence quality and whether the strategy is working.

During: “Am I testing the claim, or merely defending it?”
→

Evaluate

Compare the result with the goal, explain surprises and choose the next adjustment.

After: “What did I predict, what occurred, and why?”
01

Evidence

Which specific examples demonstrate this behaviour?

02

Consistency

How reliably do I apply it across different situations?

03

Obstacles

What makes the behaviour difficult to use?

04

Perspective

How would others describe my approach, and does that match my view?

05

Calibration

Did the confidence I expressed match the quality of my evidence and the eventual result?

06

Transfer

Where else could I use this reasoning strategy, and where might it fail?

A useful reflection record

  1. Record the decision or prediction before knowing the outcome.
  2. Give a confidence estimate and name its evidential basis.
  3. Compare the result with what you expected.
  4. Explain the gap without rewriting your original reasoning.
  5. Choose one thinking behaviour to practise next.

10

Guided transfer

Practice lab: from pattern to justified action

Critical thinking becomes transferable when you practise the same reasoning structure in a different setting. This example deliberately moves away from software delivery and shows how an analyst can avoid turning a data pattern into a premature story.

Worked example

Hospital appointments

Missed outpatient appointments rose from 7% to 10% after a new reminder system was introduced. A manager concludes: “The new system caused the increase.”
  1. Separate observation from explanation: the rate rose after the change; causation has not yet been established.
  2. Check comparability: examine clinic mix, booking lead time, patient population, season, cancellations and how the rate was calculated.
  3. Generate alternatives: messages may have failed; appointments may have been booked further ahead; one high-volume clinic may drive the change.
  4. Seek discriminating evidence: delivery logs, clinic-level trends, comparable prior periods and phased rollout groups.
  5. Act proportionately: fix confirmed delivery failures now, but use a time-limited test before replacing the whole system.
  6. Monitor consequences: track attendance, cancellations, accessibility complaints and unequal effects across patient groups.

Your turn

A manufacturing quality claim

Defects fell by 18% in the month after an AI inspection camera was installed. The supplier says the camera improved quality.

Write a short analysis that answers:

  1. What is the factual observation, and what is the causal claim?
  2. Which comparison would make the 18% meaningful?
  3. Give two competing explanations.
  4. What evidence could distinguish those explanations?
  5. What risks or affected groups must be considered?
  6. What decision could be made now, and what should be monitored?

Transfer test: a good answer does not merely ask for “more data.” It names the particular comparison or measurement that would change confidence in one explanation rather than another.

07

What competence looks like

Learning outcomes and assessment criteria

Learning outcome

Identify, analyse and solve problems systematically

The goal is not simply to name the five pillars. It is to use them as a coordinated reasoning framework in a real situation.

Evidence of competence

  • Use an appropriate systematic problem-solving technique.
  • Evaluate whether a solution was effective.
  • Identify and apply the five pillars.
  • Justify a chosen solution using logical reasoning.

08

Before you move on

Quick recall checklist

  • 01

    Can I define the problem without embedding my preferred solution?

  • 02

    Have I separated observations, interpretations and assumptions?

  • 03

    What relevant evidence or perspective is missing?

  • 04

    Does my conclusion follow logically from the evidence?

  • 05

    Can I state the claim, reasons and supporting evidence?

  • 06

    Is the explanation suited to the audience without distorting the facts?

  • 07

    Have I considered fairness, responsibility and wider consequences?

  • 08

    How will I know whether the decision worked?

The habit to keep: frame carefully, investigate openly, reason visibly, communicate clearly, act responsibly—and check what happened next.
